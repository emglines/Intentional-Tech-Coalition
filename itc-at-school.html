At School · Our 2026-27 priorities

What we’re asking of our schools this year

Families should be able to see what Educational Technology tools their children use at school, why each was chosen, and whether it is working. We ask the district to share that information and to work with families on what comes next.

Technology is already here

1:1 devices, digital learning tools, and AI are part of students' daily school experience, from elementary classrooms through CCHS.

Families can't yet see it

There is no public list of the tools students use, what data they collect, or how much time students spend on them.

The moment is now

AI is being built into Educational Technology at a fast pace, often arriving in tools students already use through routine updates. Clear guardrails set now are easier than catching up later.

Our goal: student data, attention, and well-being are protected.
Is it safe for our children?

A full tool inventory and screen time report

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask the district to publish an inventory of each student-facing EdTech tool, listing:

  • The data it collects, who it is shared with, how long it is kept, and when it is destroyed
  • Persuasive design features such as streaks or autoplay
  • Any minimum-use contract terms

We also ask for a screen time report by grade, class, and application, updated each term.

Already common in Massachusetts: see the public lists from Arlington, Northampton, Andover, and Hudson.

The student survey doesn't measure school-day screen time. The superintendent has agreed to look into it this year. Learn more

AI off by default

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask that generative AI features stay turned off for students until each one has been reviewed.

Many tools now add AI features through routine updates. Off by default means a new feature reaches students only after someone has looked at it.

Stanford's March 2026 review of more than 800 papers found no high-quality studies of student AI use in U.S. K-12 classrooms. New York City, the country's largest district, has paused student AI from its youngest grades through 8th grade for 2026-27. See the evidence

Allow-list internet

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask that student devices reach approved sites only, rather than blocking harmful sites one at a time.

  • For elementary and middle school devices
Want an allowlist for your own child now? Our K-8 guide shows how to ask for one through the internet consent form in Aspen. Read the guide
A question we hear"Shouldn't school prepare kids for AI?"

We think the best preparation is the foundation AI can't supply: reading closely, writing clearly, doing math without help, and noticing when an answer doesn't add up. AI amplifies the skills a student already has, and those skills grow best with AI out of the loop, especially in the K-8 years. That's why we ask for AI off by default, reviewed one feature at a time. More from Tech-Wise Arlington

Efficacy

Our goal: tools show evidence that they improve learning outcomes.
Does it help students learn?

Evidence at adoption

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

Before any student-facing tool is adopted, we ask the district to share:

  • The learning need it meets, and why a non-digital approach would not meet it as well
  • The research behind it: who conducted the study, which students it studied, and its ESSA evidence tier
  • Results measured by what students retain and apply away from the screen, not by time spent or engagement

Independent proof

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask that gains in learning be confirmed by independent research, not by studies funded by the company selling the product.

Fewer, better tools

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask the district to publish a count of the student-facing applications in use at each grade level this fall, both paid and free, and to reduce that list by June 2027 to only those with a proven purpose and necessity.

Alignment

Our goal: technology use reflects the district's own written goals and values.
Does it reflect our district's own goals?

Parent partnership

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask that, this year, the District (Community) Technology Committee under the Superintendent:

  • Post agendas ahead, let parent and caregiver representatives add items, and publish summaries
  • Set a timeline with clear deliverables
  • Report regularly to the School Committee on what was reviewed, decided, and why

We also ask the district to offer families, starting now, a parent and caregiver app from its filtering vendor that shows each child's screen time on school devices.

District Strategic Plan, initiative 4.4: partner with parents on best practices for student technology

Bell-to-bell policy

Asked of: School Committee

We ask the School Committee to adopt a phone policy with secure storage at all schools, ahead of state legislation, and to enforce it consistently in every building.

At the elementary schools and the Ellen Garrison Building at Concord Middle School, phones are off and in backpacks bell to bell. At Concord-Carlisle High School, they only need to be put away during class. From 2024 to 2026, sixth graders feeling depressed fell from 21% to 10%; high schoolers held at 14%. See the data

The same three questions work in any classroom

Curious about an app or program your child uses? These are the questions we find ourselves asking, and teachers often welcome them as a chance to share what they've chosen and why.

Is it safe?

What does this tool collect about my child, and can I see how much time they spend on it?

Does it help?

What is it helping my child learn, and how do you see that show up away from the screen?

Does it fit?

How does it connect to what the class, school, or district is working toward this year?
