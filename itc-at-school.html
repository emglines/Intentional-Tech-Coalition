Two kinds of tech at school

Technology is already here

1:1 devices, digital learning tools, and AI are part of students' daily school experience, from elementary classrooms through CCHS.

Families can't yet see it

There is no public list of the tools students use, what data they collect, or how much time students spend on them.

The moment is now

AI is being built into Educational Technology at a fast pace, often arriving in tools students already use through routine updates. Clear guardrails set now are easier than catching up later.

We’re asking our schools three questions about every tool our children use.

A full tool inventory and screen time report

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask the district to publish an inventory of each student-facing EdTech tool, listing:

  • The data it collects, who it is shared with, how long it is kept, and when it is destroyed
  • Persuasive design features such as streaks or autoplay
  • Any minimum-use contract terms

We also ask for a screen time report by grade, class, and application, updated each term.

Already common in Massachusetts: see the public lists from Arlington, Northampton, Andover, and Hudson.

The student survey doesn't measure school-day screen time. The superintendent has agreed to look into it this year. Learn more

AI off by default

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask that generative AI features stay turned off for students until each one has been reviewed.

Many tools now add AI features through routine updates. Off by default means a new feature reaches students only after someone has looked at it.

Stanford's March 2026 review of more than 800 papers found no high-quality studies of student AI use in U.S. K-12 classrooms. New York City, the country's largest district, has paused student AI from its youngest grades through 8th grade for 2026-27. See the evidence

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

A question we hear"Shouldn't school prepare kids for AI?"

We think the best preparation is the foundation AI can't supply: reading closely, writing clearly, doing math without help, and noticing when an answer doesn't add up. AI amplifies the skills a student already has, and those skills grow best with AI out of the loop, especially in the K-8 years. That's why we ask for AI off by default, reviewed one feature at a time. More from Tech-Wise Arlington

Efficacy

Our goal: tools show evidence that they improve learning outcomes.
Does it help students learn?

Evidence at adoption

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

Before any student-facing tool is adopted, we ask the district to share:

  • The learning need it meets, and why a non-digital approach would not meet it as well
  • The research behind it: who conducted the study, which students it studied, and its ESSA evidence tier
  • Results measured by what students retain and apply away from the screen, not by time spent or engagement

Independent proof

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask that gains in learning be confirmed by independent research, not by studies funded by the company selling the product.

Fewer, better tools

Asked of: Administration and Leadership

We ask the district to publish a count of the student-facing applications in use at each grade level this fall, both paid and free, and to reduce that list by June 2027 to only those with a proven purpose and necessity.

Bell-to-bell policy

Asked of: School Committee

We ask the School Committee to adopt a phone policy with secure storage at all schools, ahead of state legislation, and to enforce it consistently in every building.

At the elementary schools and the Ellen Garrison Building at Concord Middle School, phones are off and in backpacks bell to bell. At Concord-Carlisle High School, they only need to be put away during class. From 2024 to 2026, sixth graders feeling depressed fell from 21% to 10%; high schoolers held at 14%. Personal devices at school

Personal devices away for the whole day, and actually away

By personal devices, we mean anything internet-connected that students bring from home: mostly phones and smartwatches, but also wireless earbuds such as AirPods, and any other connected device. Concord's schools already limit phones. We ask for secure storage for all of these devices at every level, and at Concord-Carlisle High School, device-free time outside class too.

Elementary schools and the Ellen Garrison Building at Concord Middle School

Off and in backpacks, bell to bell

Phones are turned off and stay in students' backpacks for the whole school day. It's the right rule.

What's missing

A storage requirement. A device in a backpack or on a wrist is still within reach: it can buzz, it can come out in the hallway or the bathroom, and teachers are left to enforce the rule one student at a time.

89% of 6th and 8th graders know phones must be off or put away. Only 1% to 2% say phones are kept in a locker or locked box. See the data
Concord-Carlisle High School

Put away during class only

Phones need to be put away during class. At lunch, between classes, and in free periods, they're allowed.

What's missing

Device-free time outside class, and storage to back it up. Those are the hours students have to talk with each other.

77% of high schoolers describe the rule as off in class, allowed at lunch and between classes. 7% say phones are kept in a locker or locked box. See the data

Why storage matters

The rule holds

With storage, a device policy becomes a system instead of a daily negotiation. Teachers teach instead of policing, and the rule is the same in every classroom.

Attention comes back

A phone nearby draws attention even when it's turned off and face down. Out of reach is different from out of sight.

Ward et al., Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2017

Teachers see the difference

In a 2026 national study of schools that adopted phone pouches, phone use at school fell 30% by the third year, and teachers satisfied with their school's phone policy rose from 26% to 75%. (Working paper.)

Stanford, Duke, Michigan, and Penn researchers, National Bureau of Economic Research, 2026
At Concord-Carlisle High School

Free time should be time to connect

Lunch, passing time, and free periods are where friendships start, where students meet people outside their usual group, and where a ninth grader gets to know a senior. When phones and watches fill those minutes, students can sit at the same table without being together.

Belonging is improving for students overall, but LGBTQIA+ students are being left behind. Non-binary students are now almost four times as likely as their classmates to say they don't belong at school. Belonging grows in exactly those free minutes, which is why we ask that device-free time at CCHS cover the whole school day, not only class.

Massachusetts legislation

A state law is coming. Storage needs to be part of our implementation plan.

The Massachusetts Senate and House have each passed a bill requiring every public school to keep personal devices, including phones, tablets, and smartwatches, away bell to bell, including lunch and time between classes. The two versions differ, so a conference committee (a small group of senators and representatives who negotiate one final bill) has been working on it since May. Lawmakers have until early January to reach a deal.

  1. July 2025Senate passes its bill, 38 to 2
  2. April 2026House passes its version, 129 to 25
  3. May 2026Conference committee appointed
  4. NowStill negotiating, with a deal due by early January
Neither version requires storage. The Senate bill asks the state education department only to list storage "options." When a law passes, each district will decide for itself whether devices are locked away or simply put away. We ask our district to make storage part of its implementation plan, and families can help by saying so now.

Sources: Bill history and Senate text, malegislature.gov; Boston Globe, September 7, 2026

Open campuses like CCHS are already making it work

Cambridge, Massachusetts · Since fall 2025

Cambridge Rindge and Latin School

An open campus where students can leave during lunch and free periods. Phones lock in a pouch for the school day; students heading off campus unlock on the way out and lock up again when they return. Administrators said the old class-only rule cut phone use without stopping it.

Cambridge Day, August 2025; January 2026
Park City, Utah · Since fall 2025

Park City High School

Phones stay locked in pouches for the entire school day, including lunch and passing periods. Juniors and seniors with off-campus lunch privileges unlock their pouches before they leave and relock them when they return.

Park City School District
