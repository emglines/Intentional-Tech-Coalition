Three urgent steps we're asking the districts to take

From Concord and Carlisle families to Superintendent Dr. Laurie Hunter

We, the undersigned Concord Public Schools and Concord-Carlisle Regional School District families, respectfully ask the districts to take these steps while the Instructional and District (Community) Technology Committees continue their work:

  1. Move internet filtering on K-8 school-issued devices to an allowlist model by January 2027.
    Why we're asking

    A blocklist can only stop known sites, and new sites and features appear every day. With an allowlist, students would reach only websites approved for instructional use, and teachers would have a simple, timely way to request additions. An allowlist keeps children safer by limiting where they can go online. It rightly moves responsibility for staying safe online away from students and onto the systems around them. It also protects students without monitoring their activity, avoiding the kind of surveillance that can fall hardest on students who are already vulnerable. It also gives teachers a focused, predictable environment and builds on the internet safety policy the district already maintains as an E-rate recipient. Sudbury Public Schools is moving its K-8 devices to an allowlist now and will have it in place by December 2. A mid-year change is both possible and necessary.

  2. Turn off generative AI for K-8 students by January 2027, and keep it off by default.
    Why we're asking

    Vendors can add AI features to tools after the district has approved them, including tools already in classrooms. Keeping any student-facing generative AI feature off until it has been reviewed through the district's process gives the Instructional and District (Community) Technology Committees time to do their work and keeps young children's learning focused. Nearby districts have taken this step, including Lexington, Cambridge (which enables generative AI only for high school students), and Malden. In November, Google announced that its own generative AI tools will be off by default for K-12 student accounts starting early next year. A district default covers every vendor's tools and can take effect now.

  3. Complete and publish a K-12 EdTech and AI audit by March 2027.
    Why we're asking

    Following the models of Cambridge Public Schools and the Groton-Dunstable Regional School District, the audit would report how much time students spend on school-issued devices for school each day by grade, both during the school day and for homework and other school-assigned work outside school. It would show how that time divides between instructional and non-instructional use and which tools and categories of tools, including generative AI, account for it. Cambridge combined device usage data with classroom observations, teacher surveys, and student focus groups. Groton-Dunstable published median daily device use for each grade and a breakdown by type of application.

    The audit would include a public inventory of every student-facing tool in use. More than 50 Massachusetts districts, including Lexington, Wayland, and Westford, already publish their inventories through the Student Data Privacy Consortium, listing each tool's purpose, the grades and subjects that use it, the student data it collects, and its signed data privacy agreement. Each district's inventory would follow the same format, mark which tools are currently in use, and note any minimum-use contract terms.

Thank you for your leadership and your partnership with Concord and Carlisle families.
